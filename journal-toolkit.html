Eight practices that make a journal stronger

The goal is a publication that is fair to contributors, meaningful for student editors, accessible to readers, and possible for next year's team to inherit.

1. Make the journal a student learning experience

When possible, anchor the journal in a course, practicum, club, or clearly defined editorial team. California examples at Reedley, Orange Coast, Sacramento City, CCSF, and San Bernardino Valley College connect publication work to student learning in selection, editing, design, production, publicity, and events.

2. Set selection criteria before reading submissions

Agree on what strong work means for each genre before the staff begins voting. A short rubric or editorial criteria sheet makes discussion more consistent and helps students explain decisions using craft rather than personal taste alone.

3. Use blind review when it is practical

Remove names from work during first-round review when your process allows it, and create a conflict-of-interest rule for editors who recognize a submission. San Bernardino Valley College's Phineas, for example, explicitly uses blind review.

4. Make submission rules easy to understand

State who may submit, accepted genres, limits, file types, reading period, simultaneous-submission rules, previously published rules, response expectations, rights, fees, and your AI/authorship policy. Put the important rules on the page, not only inside a form.

5. Protect writers' rights and trust

Say clearly what publication permission the journal needs and what the writer keeps. Avoid vague claims to ownership. If you request first-publication rights, explain when those rights revert to the creator and how future reprints should credit the journal.

6. Build accessibility into the issue, not onto it later

Use real headings, meaningful links, readable contrast, alt text for art and photography, keyboard-friendly web pages, captions or transcripts for media, and accessible PDFs or HTML. Do not make a visual flipbook the only way to read the issue.

7. Leave a handoff package for the next staff

Keep an editorial calendar, style sheet, selection rubric, contributor templates, production checklist, accessible design notes, vendor details, and a record of what worked. Store institutional credentials in an approved shared location rather than a student's personal account.

8. Treat the launch as part of the publication

A reading, launch event, web feature, classroom visit, or social campaign gives contributors an audience and helps the journal recruit the next generation of writers and editors. Build outreach into the production calendar instead of adding it at the last minute.

A simple annual rhythm

Make the workflow visible

  1. Before submissions open: confirm eligibility, genres, dates, rights language, AI policy, review criteria, accessibility needs, and staff roles.
  2. During the call: promote beyond creative writing classes, answer questions consistently, and track outreach so students across programs know they are invited.
  3. After the deadline: anonymize first-round submissions when feasible, apply agreed criteria, document decisions, and protect contributor information.
  4. Editing and production: obtain permissions, copyedit collaboratively, proof contributor names and titles carefully, add alt text, and test the digital issue with keyboard and zoom.
  5. Publication and launch: publish in an accessible format, celebrate contributors, archive the issue, and make past issues easy to find.
  6. Handoff: record deadlines, templates, lessons learned, and next steps while the process is still fresh.

Quick benchmark

A four-question self-check

The Community College Humanities Association's current literary magazine competition offers a useful lens. Before you publish, ask:

  • Does the issue look intentional and inviting?
  • Is the editing accurate and consistent?
  • Can readers navigate it easily?
  • Does the selected student work feel strong and thoughtfully curated?

Submission Guidelines Checklist

Who + what

  • Eligibility
  • Accepted genres
  • Number/length limits
  • File types
  • Language/translation policy

When + how

  • Opening and closing dates
  • Submission method
  • Expected response window
  • Simultaneous submissions
  • How to withdraw work

Trust + permissions

  • Previously published policy
  • Copyright/rights language
  • AI/authorship policy
  • Fees, if any
  • Privacy/contact-use statement
Brain-friendly rule: if a student has to open three PDFs, decode publishing jargon, and email someone just to learn whether they are eligible, the guidelines are doing too much work.

Accessibility

A literary journal is both art and information

Beautiful design and accessibility are not opposites. The strongest digital issue gives readers multiple ways into the work.

For the website and submission call

  • Use semantic headings in a logical order.
  • Use descriptive link text instead of “click here.”
  • Label every form control.
  • Keep text contrast strong and never communicate status by color alone.
  • Make every action usable by keyboard.

For the published issue

  • Add meaningful alt text to informative art and photographs.
  • Use actual text instead of images of text whenever possible.
  • Provide captions/transcripts for audio and video.
  • If you use a flipbook, also provide an accessible HTML or tagged-PDF path.
  • Test at high zoom and on a phone before launch.

Research and models

Examples worth studying

The recommendations above combine current community college models with independent-publishing ethics and W3C accessibility guidance.

  • Orange Coast College, “ENGL A011: Intro to Literary Magazine Production” — detailed course model covering selection criteria, editorial policy, style sheets, editing, production, publicity, and representation.
  • Reedley College, Kings River Review — a student-created journal produced through ENGL 15J, Literary Journal Publication.
  • Sacramento City College, “College Literary Magazine” and “College Literary Magazine: Production” — a two-course model that separates editorial work from production and public reading.
  • San Bernardino Valley College, Phineas Literary Magazine — student production with explicitly blind submission review.
  • Community College Humanities Association, “Literary Magazine Competition” — current evaluation criteria for community college literary magazines.
  • Community of Literary Magazines and Presses, “CLMP Contest Code of Ethics” — emphasizes clear rules, transparency, and ethical administration.
  • Poets & Writers, “Copyright Information for Writers” — plain-language explanation of publication rights.
  • W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, “Designing for Web Accessibility” — contrast, non-color cues, navigation, labels, responsive design, and media alternatives.

Research reviewed September 2026. Practices should be adapted to local college policy, student privacy requirements, accessibility procedures, and available staffing.
